🤔 Isn't this more work than counting?

Yes. Counting reads the text once. Learning goes through all 46 pairs, fixes every score a little, and repeats that 300 times.

For this small table, counting is quicker. But the smarter model in Lesson 5 has numbers that can't be counted, and learning still works there.

Here we can check it: learning ends with the same chances as counting.

Learning repeats 4 steps:

  1. a)Predict the next character
  2. b)Measure how wrong
  3. c)Find which way to change each score
  4. d)Move each score a little

To predict, your model needs chances, not scores. How do scores become chances?

Scores into chances

Step a) Predict

To guess, your model needs chances that add up to 1, like the rows of the counting table.

These are your model's random scores for row t. Drag h up. Watch the others shrink.
You can drag 5 of the 14 scores. The other 9 letters keep their scores and are added together at the bottom.

Each score becomes escore: always positive, and a bigger score gives a much bigger result. Then each one is divided by the total, so all the chances add up to 1.

e is a fixed number, about 2.718.

Any scores in, chances out. They always add up to 1, and raising one score takes chance away from the others.

This is softmax.

🤔 Why escore? Why not just divide the scores by their total?

Because scores can be negative. Row t starts with s at −2.05, and all 14 scores add up to 0.95. Dividing gives −2.05 ÷ 0.95 = −216%. A chance can't be negative.

escore is always positive: e−2.05 = 0.13. And a higher score still gives a bigger number, so the order stays the same.

🤔 Why e, and not 2 or 10?

With e, a small nudge to the score grows escore by exactly that much, as a percentage. Nudge a score up by 0.01:

21 = 2.000→ 21.01 = 2.0140.69% bigger
e1 = 2.718→ e1.01 = 2.7461.00% bigger
101 = 10.00→ 101.01 = 10.232.30% bigger

With e, nudge and growth are the same number. With 2 or 10, every nudge gets an extra 0.69 or 2.30.

Learning keeps asking “if I nudge this score, how much does the chance change?”. With e there's no extra number, so the answer is the simple rule two pages on: move each score by “chance − goal”.

2 or 10 would still work: your model would just learn bigger or smaller scores, and the chances would come out the same. e only keeps the math cleanest.

🤔 Why is it called softmax?

Max means: pick the biggest score, give it everything. Soft means: not all-or-nothing.

Row t's three highest scores: “.” 1.35, l 1.31, h 1.07.

.lh
max100%0%0%
softmax17.7%16.9%13.3%

Max gives l nothing, though its score is almost the same as “.”. Softmax gives the biggest score the most, but shares the rest by how close each one is.

Make the gaps bigger (all scores × 10) and softmax hardens towards max: “.” 58.9%, l 36.7%, h 3.4%. So softmax is a softened max.

The less chance your model gives the right answer, the bigger the loss. Give it 100% and the loss is 0.

The rule your model uses: loss = −log(chance)

🤔 What is log?

You don't need the math. −log just turns a chance into a “how wrong” number:

100% → 0 (perfect), 90% → 0.11, 50% → 0.69, 13.3% → 2.02, 1% → 4.61.

The smaller the chance, the bigger the number.

That was just one pair. Every pair has its own right answer: in “cat ”, t is followed by a space, so there the right answer is space.

Your model does this for every pair in the text: 46 pairs, but only 24 different ones. A pair that repeats uses the same box every time, so it has the same loss every time, and it counts each time it appears. With its real random scores:

One number for how far your model's guesses are from the real text.

The goal is to make the loss small. But which of the 196 scores should change, and which way?

Which way?

Steps c) Find directions and d) Move

After t, our text has h 4 times, space 3 times and a full stop once.

To see how one score moves, start with just one pair: t → h. (The next page uses all 8.)

For this pair the right answer is h, and your model gave it only 13.3%. To make this pair's loss smaller, h needs a bigger chance.

The chances come from the scores in row t (softmax: a higher score gives a higher chance). So we change the scores:

  • h's score goes up, so h's chance grows.
  • Every other letter's score goes down, so their chances shrink. The chances always add up to 100%, so what they lose goes to h.

Even space and full stop go down here, because for this one pair they're wrong. When all 8 pairs count together (next page), their own pairs push them back up.

For each score, one number tells us which way to move it, and by how much. That number is its gradient: gradient = chance − goal.

The goal is what the chance should be. For this one pair, the right answer h should get everything (goal 1), and every other letter nothing (goal 0). (13.3% is written as 0.133.)

  • h: 0.133 − 1 = −0.867
  • Full stop: 0.177 − 0 = +0.177
  • Space: 0.074 − 0 = +0.074

To move a score: new score = old score − gradient.

  • h: 1.069 − (−0.867) = 1.936. Taking away a negative number adds it, so h's score goes up.
  • Full stop: 1.354 − 0.177 = 1.177. A positive gradient makes the score go down.

So the + or − tells the direction, and the distance from 0 tells how much: h (−0.867, far from 0) moves a lot, space (+0.074, close to 0) hardly moves.

🤔 Why “chance − goal”?

It's the gap between what your model gives and what it should give. Below the goal, the gap is negative, so the score goes up. Above it, positive, so it goes down. At the goal it's 0: nothing to fix.

If your model already gave h 1 (100%): 1 − 1 = 0, so the score doesn't move. It gave only 0.133: 0.133 − 1 = −0.867, far from the goal, so it moves up a lot.

These arrows are the gradients. Why is the goal the share? Each pair gives its own gradient (goal 1 for its right answer, 0 for the rest), and the 8 are averaged. For space (chance 0.074):

  • the 4 pairs t → h: space is wrong, so 0.074 − 0 = +0.074 each
  • the 3 pairs t → ␣: space is right, so 0.074 − 1 = −0.926 each
  • the 1 pair t → .: space is wrong, so 0.074 − 0 = +0.074

Average: (4 × 0.074 + 3 × (−0.926) + 0.074) ÷ 8 = −0.30. Negative, so space goes up. On the last page, with only t → h, it went down.

And that's exactly chance − share: 0.074 − 3/8 = −0.30. Averaging the 1s and 0s of the 8 pairs gives the share.

But notice the effort: counting needed one pass through the text. Learning needed 300 passes to reach the same chances. For this small table, counting is quicker.

So why learn? Learning never asks “how many times did this pair appear?”. It only asks “should this score go up or down?”. That is why it will still work in Lesson 5, where the numbers can't be filled by counting.

Here's all of it in Python.

The code

Guess what each print shows. Then press ▶ Run.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

text = "the cat sat on the mat. the dog sat on the log."
unique_chars = sorted(set(text))
vocab_size = len(unique_chars)
char_to_number = {}
number_to_char = {}
for number, char in enumerate(unique_chars):
    char_to_number[char] = number
    number_to_char[number] = char

# Pairs: this character, and the next one
current_chars = []
next_chars = []
for i in range(len(text) - 1):
    current_chars.append(char_to_number[text[i]])
    next_chars.append(char_to_number[text[i + 1]])
current_chars = torch.tensor(current_chars)
next_chars = torch.tensor(next_chars)

# The model: random scores. requires_grad=True lets PyTorch find gradients.
torch.manual_seed(42)
weights = torch.randn((vocab_size, vocab_size), requires_grad=True)

learning_rate = 10
for step in range(300):
    scores = weights[current_chars]                # a) predict
    loss = F.cross_entropy(scores, next_chars)     # b) measure
    weights.grad = None
    loss.backward()                                # c) find directions
    weights.data = weights.data - learning_rate * weights.grad   # d) move
    if step % 50 == 0:
        print("Step", step, "loss:", round(loss.item(), 3))

# What did it learn after 't'?
probabilities = F.softmax(weights, dim=1)
t_row = probabilities[char_to_number['t']]
for char in [' ', '.', 'h']:
    print(repr(char), round(t_row[char_to_number[char]].item(), 3))

🤔 What does each line in the training loop do?
  • weights[current_chars]: copies the row of each of the 46 characters.
  • F.cross_entropy: softmax, pick the right answer's chance, −log, average.
  • weights.grad = None: clears old gradients. PyTorch adds new ones on top.
  • loss.backward(): fills weights.grad with a gradient for every score.
  • weights.data - learning_rate * weights.grad: the minus flips the sign, so negative goes up.
